UX Research + Strategic Analysis

AI Adoption & Trust in Digital Banking

A synthesis of published research pointed to three design priorities for AI-assisted banking: explain the action, give people a chance to review it, and preserve their ability to override it. These are strategic recommendations, not findings from participant testing.

the business problem

Overview

The project investigated how AI-driven features influence user trust in digital banking and what design principles could guide AI integration without undermining user confidence. The study synthesized public market research and academic literature, then mapped the findings into behavioral archetypes, journeys, competitive examples, and strategic UX principles.

Research approach: secondary research synthesis → thematic clustering → insight synthesis → opportunity mapping.

This was a synthesis of published research, not a participant study. Provider examples and survey figures below link to their original publications; the design implications are my interpretation of that evidence.

what the sources support and what still needs testing

Evidence & Strategy Hypotheses

01

Comfort depends on the task

In TD Bank's 2025 U.S. survey, 60% were comfortable using AI for budgeting, compared with 44% for investing. This suggests that confidence varies with the decision being delegated. TD Bank survey ↗

02

Human oversight remains important

The same survey describes trust, transparency, and keeping people involved as foundations for AI in banking. This supports review and confirmation steps before consequential actions. TD Bank survey ↗

03

Explanation and override are design hypotheses

The synthesis translates the trust concerns into proposed explanations, review steps, and override controls. Their effect on actual banking behavior still needs to be tested with users.

04

Make the benefit visible, then test it

Showing a concrete outcome, such as money saved, is a proposed way to make an AI feature easier to evaluate. This study did not test alternative messages or measure engagement.

two hypothesis-generating frames

Behavioral Archetypes

Jonah and Taylor are illustrative archetypes synthesized from secondary research. Their statements are written examples, not participant quotes.

Digitally Native — Jonah Johnson

Values: speed + control

“I want AI to handle the routine stuff so I can focus on decisions that matter.”

  • Adopts AI when it reduces friction and confirms actions without slowing the task down.
  • Comfortable automating recurring payments.
  • Frustrated by unnecessary confirmation steps or opaque delays.

Trust trigger: demonstrable speed advantage with a visible override option.

Digitally Wary — Taylor Morgan

Values: safety + transparency

“I need to understand what’s happening with my money before I let a machine touch it.”

  • Prefers AI that explains before it acts.
  • Adopts when steps are clear and an easy opt-out exists.
  • Distrusts recommendations without rationale.

Trust trigger: plain-language explanation of logic with a confirm-before-action pattern.

Competitive Benchmarking

These are provider-reported examples, not measured outcomes of this research. Each claim links to the provider's publication.

TD Bank — Personalization & Analytics

TD said its AI Prism model processes 100 times more data variables to help predict customer needs. At its 2025 launch, personalized marketing was a planned use, not a measured engagement result. TD announcement ↗

Bank of America — Conversational AI + Internal Knowledge

Bank of America reported more than 3 billion Erica interactions and said over 98% of users find the information they need. It separately reported that AskGPS uses more than 3,200 internal documents to answer employee questions within seconds. Erica report ↗ AskGPS report ↗

Visa — Fraud Detection

Visa reported that its AI fraud solutions helped banks detect and stop more than $40 billion in card payment fraud in 2023. That figure covers Visa's AI fraud solutions, rather than Advanced Authorization alone. Visa report ↗

Stripe — Payment Acceptance

Stripe describes Adaptive Acceptance as an AI-powered product designed to recover false declines and improve payment acceptance. Stripe technical article ↗

design ideas to validate next

Strategic UX Implications

Explainable AI

Offer a plain-language reason for an AI recommendation, with more detail available when users want it.

Confirmation Checkpoints

Let users review consequential AI-initiated actions before execution. Test whether this improves their understanding and sense of control.

Real-Time Validation

Show the result after an AI action completes so users can check what happened and whether it helped.

Progressive Disclosure

Lead with the insight, offer detail on tap, and reserve technical specifics for users who seek them. Do not front-load complexity.

User Override Controls

Provide a clear path to undo, adjust, or opt out of AI recommendations, and test whether people can find and use those controls.

the sentence I wanted people to remember

Core Takeaway

This synthesis suggests a design hypothesis: people need to see, understand, and retain control over AI-assisted financial actions. User testing is the next step to learn whether the proposed patterns earn that trust.

where the evidence stops

Limitations

  • No primary behavioral data: findings were derived from published industry reports and competitive analysis.
  • Publication bias: provider reports describe their own products and may emphasize favorable outcomes.
  • Archetypes are illustrative: Jonah and Taylor were synthesized from secondary research patterns and were not validated through primary interviews.
  • Rapidly evolving landscape: the original research and linked 2025 examples may not reflect newer AI features or expectations.
