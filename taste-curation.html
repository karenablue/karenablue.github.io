Taste Curation: How People Curate Personal Taste Online

Participants could discover inspiration, but struggled to organize and retrieve what they had saved. Contextual observation and interviews traced the gap between collecting content and using it later.

the problem happens after “save”

Executive Summary

The study found that discovery itself was not the core unmet need. Participants could find inspiration across social platforms, search engines, and e-commerce. The breakdown appeared later: saved content became difficult to organize, remember, retrieve, and use.

Core unmet need: a centralized system with cross-platform saving, flexible tagging, and intelligent resurfacing that helps users move from passive accumulation to intentional curation.

the observed behavior flow

Taste Curation Sequence Model

Trigger

Assigned apartment decorating task.

Select starting platform

Search engine, social media, or e-commerce — usually chosen based on habit.

Browse results visually

Participants scanned quickly and made fast aesthetic judgments.

Quick like / dislike judgment

Disliked items returned the participant to browsing. Liked items could be saved.

Save item

Participants used carts, folders, boards, playlists, screenshots, or collections.

Continue browsing?

Yes: loop back into browsing.

No: attempt to review saved content.

Review saved content?

Some participants: reflect on saved items and identify patterns.

Many participants: struggle with decision friction and remain unable to translate inspiration into a clear direction.

Interview themes

01

Taste is emotional, evolving, and validation-seeking

Participants described taste as unstable and identity-linked rather than fixed.

02

Discovery is algorithm-driven and overwhelming

Recommendation systems accelerated exposure while also producing repetition, distrust, and overload.

03

Saving is easy, but organization is fragmented

Saved content lived across many platforms and often lacked a structure that scaled with volume.

04

Resurfacing is the core breakdown point

Participants struggled to reconnect saved content with present decisions and frequently started over.

three different ways the same problem shows up

Personas

Maya · 25 · Graduate Student / Part-Time Worker

Digital comfort: 5/5

Primary platforms: TikTok, Instagram, Pinterest

Uses TikTok broadly for recipes, outfit ideas, parties, apartments, and inspiration. Saves quickly when something catches her eye.

Frustrations: losing inspiration she worked to find; unhelpful thumbnails; AI-generated content mixed into feeds.

Needs: proactive resurfacing, multi-category tagging, and notes at the moment of saving.

Marcus · 24 · Product Engineer

Digital comfort: 5/5

Primary platforms: TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, Reddit, Google

Approaches inspiration methodically and saves material as a long-term archive of future decisions and aspirations.

Frustrations: content scattered across platforms; re-searching because old saves cannot be located; knowing information exists but being unable to use it.

Needs: cross-platform saving, tagging/search, and goal-tied resurfacing.

Darius · 22 · Sales Agent

Digital comfort: 5/5

Primary platforms: TikTok, Instagram, Amazon, Facebook

Does not think of himself as a “curator.” Browses, saves to cart, checks practical constraints, and decides near the point of purchase.

Frustrations: ending up with things that are “fine”; forgetting why something was saved; no easy side-by-side comparison at decision time.

Needs: decision support at purchase time, quick comparison, and a simple way to revisit intent.

what retrieval friction does to motivation

Scenarios & Customer Journeys

Maya — themed party planning

Stage What happens Effect
Collect Saves about 15 TikTok videos across three folders. Feels good about the direction.
Return Two weeks later, thumbnails look similar; Pinterest and Instagram saves cannot be located. Rewatches content and searches again.
Decision Loses about an hour and chooses what is easiest to find. Motivation drops and the result does not fully match her original vision.

Marcus — long-term car research

Stage What happens Effect
Collect Saves YouTube reviews, Reddit threads, screenshots, and TikTok comparisons over months. Feels prepared and informed.
Return Saved material is mixed with unrelated content and lacks searchable structure. Cannot reconstruct the research trail.
Decision Spends about two hours piecing research together, then starts new searches. Shortlist is based on what is easy to find, not the months of careful comparison.

Darius — couch purchase

Stage What happens Effect
Collect Adds several options to a shopping cart. No strong emotional investment; this is just how shopping works.
Return Cannot remember why one option was saved. Deletes it and narrows by price and ratings.
Decision Makes the choice entirely at purchase time with little context from earlier browsing. Often buys things that are “fine” instead of things he genuinely loves.

what the research points toward

Design Implications

Cross-platform saving + search

The most consistent unmet need was the ability to retrieve saved inspiration across platforms rather than remember where it lives.

Context-aware resurfacing

Saved content should return when it becomes relevant instead of remaining buried in a collection.

Multi-category tagging

Allow one item to belong to more than one context, addressing the single-collection limitation participants described.

Reflection at the moment of saving

Lightweight context — why this mattered, what it is for — can make later retrieval and decision-making more meaningful.

where the findings stop

Limitations

  • Observation sessions lasted 30 minutes to 1 hour, which is short relative to the long-term development of personal taste.
  • The observation sample skewed younger: 6 of 8 participants were younger adults.
  • The interview sample also consisted largely of digitally comfortable adults.
  • Different platforms expose different saving, collection, and resurfacing affordances.
  • The final report recommends more demographically diverse and longitudinal research.

Key takeaway: the study began as a question about discovery and collection, but the evidence shifted the problem toward retrieval. The value of a saved item depends on whether a person can reconnect it to the decision they are making later.
